## 1. MyCycle

**Say:** MyCycle is a screening aid. It learns what is normal for one person and notices when that person's pattern changes in a way that looks like PCOS, anemia or thyroid trouble. It never gives a diagnosis; it always suggests seeing a doctor.

## 2. The problem: four barriers stack up

**Say:** Four things stack on top of each other: tests are costly or far away, myths fill the information gap, people do not trust apps with intimate data, and taboo stops the conversation. MyCycle attacks the first three with software.

## 3. What existing systems do, and what is missing

**Say:** Existing apps use one fixed range for everyone and keep raw data on their servers. Existing research gets high accuracy only with lab tests. What is missing is screening that is personal, needs no lab, and keeps data on the phone. These are feature and method differences; we do not claim to be more accurate than any named app.

## 4. What the product does (and what is built today)

**Say:** The engine is built and measured: the detector, the models, the private learning, and the backend. The phone app screens are still being built. Everything you see in this demo runs the real code, not mock-ups.

## 5. The form: 11 questions the detector uses today

**Say:** She fills one short form per period. Each question exists because it feeds one of the signals the detector watches. We ask for dates, not cycle length, because people misremember cycle length.

## 6. How pain and flow are measured, so a number means the same to everyone

**Say:** Sir's question was fair: what does pain 6 mean? On our form every number has a meaning written next to it. 4 to 6 means she had to slow down or skip some things. Flow is described by what she sees on the pad, not by words like heavy.

## 7. What we add next, and what we never ask

**Say:** Next we want to ask whether anything unusual happened, like travel or exams, because a single stressful month is the main source of false alarms. And we deliberately do not ask about sex or discharge: not needed for these screenings, and too sensitive on a shared phone.

## 8. Questions about the form, answered

**Say:** What exactly does pain 6 mean? It is her worst period pain this cycle on the validated 0 to 10 menstrual pain scale (0 = No pain, 10 = Extremely severe). At 4 to 6 the label reads "I had to slow down or skip some things", so two women mean the same thing. A change of about 1 point is considered meaningful (NRS). Is pain one number for the whole cycle? Yes: the worst day of this cycle. The validated scale also allows average and current pain; we use worst because it is easiest to remember once a month and it is what a doctor asks first. How do you measure "heavy" flow without a lab? Each level is described by what she sees on the pad or tampon. An optional counting chart (the standard pictorial blood loss chart) turns pads and clots into a score on the phone. Why 0 to 3 for symptoms and not 0 to 10? Four answers with plain-language labels are quicker and more reliable for low-literacy users than an 11-point scale. Each label says how the symptom affected her day. Why do you ask for dates and not cycle length? People misremember cycle length. Two start dates give the exact number.

## 9. How it works: two tiers on the phone, only a protected update leaves

**Say:** Everything that touches your data happens on the phone. Tier 1 is simple statistics that track your own normal. A rule layer turns what changed into one of five flags. The only thing that can ever leave the phone is a clipped, noise-added model update, and only if you switch it on.

## 10. Federated learning in plain words

**Say:** Instead of collecting everyone's data in one place, each phone learns a little from its own data, sends only a small protected update, the server averages the updates, and sends a better model back. Your raw logs never travel.

## 11. Algorithms used, named

**Say:** Every piece has a name. The detector is a hybrid of two classic change detectors, a z-score and CUSUM, plus rules that name the pattern. Learning across phones is FedAvg with differential privacy.

## 12. The formulas, with every symbol explained

**Say:** z-score: how far this cycle is from your usual, in units of your usual spread. CUSUM: a running total of small excesses, so a slow drift adds up until it crosses a limit. Clipping caps how much one person can move the model; noise hides any single person. FedAvg is a weighted average.

## 13. Words you will hear, in plain language

## 29. Personal baseline vs one fixed range for everyone

**Say:** A fixed range judges everyone against the average woman. A woman whose normal cycle is 37 days gets flagged every single month, even though nothing is wrong for her (our tests check exactly this case). The personal baseline learns her own normal. Measured: it catches more thyroid-like changes (99% vs 85%) and flags fewer healthy people, but on this simulator the fixed range raises slightly fewer false alarms per cycle (3.36 vs 4.27). Most simulated women have normal cycles well inside 21 to 35 days, which flatters the fixed range. The advantage of a personal baseline should be larger for women whose normal sits near the edges; we have not measured that subgroup separately yet.

## 30. Which change detector for Tier 1

**Say:** A z-score alone reacts to sudden jumps but misses slow creeps (it caught only 36% of anemia-like changes). CUSUM catches slow creeps but reacts later. The hybrid combines both and catches PCOS-like changes in the same cycle they start. But the EWMA chart alone does about as well with fewer false alarms (2.92 vs 4.27) and flags fewer healthy people (20% vs 28%). On these numbers the EWMA chart is a credible replacement for the hybrid. We are reporting that, and it is an open decision for the team.

## 31. Neural network (GRU) vs simple statistics for spotting change

**Say:** Neural networks need lots of examples per person. Each simulated person has only 16 to 30 cycles, so simple statistics that track one person's average beat all three neural networks. GRU, LSTM and CNN were almost identical to each other. With much longer histories, or real data with richer patterns, a neural network could catch up. The GRU is kept, exported and tested, but it does not raise flags.

## 32. Which screening model (symptoms to risk)

**Say:** Eight models scored almost the same on symptoms alone, so the data sets the ceiling, not the model. Random forest is near the top everywhere and is the significant winner in two comparisons. It also shows which symptoms mattered and needs no special hardware. The deep neural network was the weakest, which is typical for small tables of data. On symptoms alone k-NN was fractionally higher and the difference is not significant; any of the top four would be a defensible choice.

## 33. Symptoms only vs using lab tests

**Say:** Lab tests make the model better (AUC 0.96 vs 0.89), but anyone who already has those tests has seen a doctor. The point of MyCycle is to nudge people before that step, at home, for free. We accept the lower accuracy and state it. Symptom-only screening will miss some cases; that is why every flag says consider consulting a doctor, and a quiet result is not an all-clear.

## 34. Where the learning happens

**Say:** Pooling everyone's data gives the best accuracy but puts intimate data on a server. Keeping it on each phone alone is private but weak (AUC 0.74). Federated learning keeps data on the phone and gets 0.88, close to pooling. Measured with 8 simulated phones on the PCOS dataset, not real phones; real networks add dropouts and delays.

## 35. Which federated method

**Say:** FedProx ranked slightly better (AUC 0.89 vs 0.88) but got far fewer answers right at the default cut-off (68% vs 83%). FedPer was weakest. FedAvg is the simplest, most standard method and the best balance here. One run per setting on a small dataset; FedProx's AUC edge could be real. SCAFFOLD and FedAdam were not run.

## 36. Adding differential privacy

**Say:** Without noise, a clever server could learn things about one person from her update. Differential privacy adds noise so that any one person barely changes the result, with a measured budget, epsilon. It costs accuracy, and we show exactly how much. Secure aggregation and homomorphic encryption are other ways to hide updates; we did not test them. DP gives a bounded leak, never zero.

## 37. Personalizing the neural network per user

**Say:** Tuning the model on a user's first 4 cycles made it catch more changes, but it also started crying wolf far more often. Too many false alarms make people ignore flags, so we do not use it. With more cycles for fine-tuning, or a false-alarm penalty during tuning, this could improve.

## 38. Filling in missing answers

**Say:** Only 2 of 541 records had a missing answer, so both methods give the same result. We use the simpler one. On data with many gaps (real app logs), this choice would matter more and should be re-tested.

## 39. Indian survey (NFHS-5) vs US survey (NHANES) for anemia and thyroid

**Say:** Our users are Indian women. A US survey would describe a different population, diet and anemia rate. NFHS-5 is India's own survey with real blood measurements. We did not run NHANES, so this is a reasoned choice, not a measured comparison.

## 44. Questions we expect, answered

**Say:** Is this a diagnosis? No. It raises a screening flag and always says consider consulting a doctor. Only a doctor diagnoses. Why use a simulator instead of real data? No public dataset follows the same woman's cycles for years with a diagnosis attached, for privacy reasons. The simulator gives us people where the true answer is known, and its normal cycles match 159 real women closely. Did you train on millions of rows? Real data: 541 PCOS patients and 126,625 women in NFHS-5. The simulator can generate millions of rows, always labeled synthetic. We do not mix the two. How much accuracy do you lose without lab tests? AUC 0.96 with labs vs 0.89 with symptoms only. That is the honest price of screening at home.

## 45. Questions we expect, answered

**Say:** Why not deep learning? We tried it. The deep network was the weakest of 8 screening models (AUC 0.86), and the GRU was weaker than simple statistics for spotting change. Small data favours simple models. Why federated learning? Each phone alone reaches only AUC 0.74. Federated learning reaches 0.88, close to pooling everything (0.89), without raw data leaving any phone. What does differential privacy actually protect? It limits how much any one person can change what the server learns, measured by epsilon (for example 4.3 at noise 1.0). It is a bounded leak, not zero leakage. How do you know there is no data leakage? Every lab and scan column was removed from the symptom models, cleaning is learned inside each training fold, and our code refuses to tune thresholds on the test data.
